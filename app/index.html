About this project

There is growing interest in "synthetic user" research, where LLMs are used to simulate responses to interviews or surveys. It is usually unclear what data they draw on or how it has been processed .

This project tests a more transparent alternative. Instead of asking an LLM to "simulate" a person, I am using LLMs to process and synthesize patterns from high quality survey data. I am using the SIPHER dataset (derived from the UK Census and the UK Household Longitudinal Study) to generate representative personas at the local authority level. My goal is to create a tool where researchers can see how data is processed.

This prototype currently shows six local authorities – running the processing pipeline is moderately expensive so I've limited the scope. I've demonstrated four approaches to using LLMs to create 'personas'.

See my Research Design and Methodology to learn more about my approach to building accountable AI for public sector research.

Research design

Hypothesis

LLM assisted processing, filtering and presentation of social research data in a transparent, explainable way can provide actionable user insights that could be more accountable in the public sector than black box 'synthetic user' research tools.

Next steps

  • Initial demos of the prototype indicate that 'employment type by local authority' is not an appropriate population segmentation for common research objectives.
  • Responding to this, I would like to allow the user more control over the sub-population that they cluster on to create 'personas'.
  • Identifying real-world projects for qualitative concept testing — are data-led personas a useful output? Do people naturally gravitate to 'simulated users' instead? Is the data resolution good enough? What other datasets should be added?

Broader research questions

Transparency & Explainability

  • How do users interpret the "reasoning" provided by the tool?
  • How can explanations of how the data is derived provide transparency without overwhelming?
  • How are limitations communicated?
  • How can we detect, evaluate and communicate biases introduced by the LLM?
  • Does the tool introduce new types of errors that users are not accustomed to managing?
  • Evaluate and communicate LLM biases

Utility

  • Where would this fit into a research process?
  • How do outcomes compare with traditional research methods in terms of accuracy and relevance?
  • How does it compare to 'black box' synthetic user tools in terms of accuracy and relevance?
  • Where could this alternative to synthetic user research be most applicable and compelling?

Detailed methodology

This experiment uses the Sipher dataset. Sipher is a 'synthetic population'. For every MSOA, it provides a list of IDs that link to respondents in the UK Household Longitudinal Study (UKHLS). The demographics (age, sex, ethnicity, education, employment status, marital status, housing status and self-reported health) of the UKHLS respondents are selected so that the overall synthetic population of the MSOA matches the outcome of the last census. This means that individuals in the population are real people, but they are most likely not from the MSOA in question, providing a strong degree of anonymity. It also means that statistical features of the population at MSOA level — averages and distributions — match the census. The UKHLS has about 20,000 respondents, so each respondent is represented in the synthetic dataset many times.

Step 1 — Construct the population

The synthetic population is constructed by connecting the Sipher ID lists back to the UKHLS data.

Step 2 — Group MSOAs into LAs

Respondent records are aggregated from MSOA to Local Authority level.

Step 3 — Group by employment status within each LA

A process of hierarchical clustering is applied to the Sipher data. Within each LA, the population is grouped by employment status. This is because clustering the whole LA is too computationally difficult for LLMs.

Step 4 — Cluster

For each employment status group, a clustering process is applied. Three approaches to clustering are presented in the UI:

  • K-means clustering on simplified demographics (the raw data is too high-dimensional for reasonable clustering).
  • Vector embedding clustering. Convert each profile into a string, embed using text-embedding-3-small, then cluster on the vectors.
  • Claude and Gemini clustering. The demographic data of 100 UKHLS respondents are sent to the LLM with a prompt that asks for between three and eight clusters to be identified. The 100 respondents are selected as the most common within the employment status group for that LA. Because Sipher is a synthetic dataset, an individual respondent can appear in the data many times, so the top 100 respondents might represent between 3,000 and 10,000 people.

Step 5 — Calculate average demographics per cluster

The cards in the UI are generated by calculating the average demographics of each cluster.
